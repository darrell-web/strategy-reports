SECTION 01

The question on the table

Ownership's concern
"Are First Saturday events acquiring new customers — or are repeat customers just using them to get extended discounts on items they would normally purchase at full price?"

It's the right question to ask. And it has a clean per-store answer. Six months of First Saturday data across all three stores — cross-validated against 14 months of POS — reveal that the same event mechanic is producing fundamentally different economic outcomes at each location. The owners' concern is empirically wrong at one store and empirically correct at two.

This deck answers the cadence question — monthly, quarterly, or semi-annual — in dollars, in new-customer counts, and in tradable margin per event.

The clearest piece of evidence — owner's concern empirically fails at Sacramento

Sacramento First Saturday delivers 11 incremental new customers per event vs. a baseline day (26.5 vs. 15.5). That's not within statistical noise — it's a clean +71% lift. The event is doing what it's designed to do.

The hidden March-discount finding (this is the actionable insight)

The 4-month Feb → Apr arc is effectively a controlled experiment. Same store, same event mechanic, only the discount knob changed:

March's deeper discount produced the worst outcome on every dimension. Fewer new customers, lowest margin, longest waits, lowest gross income. The Sacramento First Saturday has hit its discount-elasticity ceiling. Sacramento can run the event at 22–23% discount and capture more new customers AND more margin AND faster service than at 25–26%. Going deeper is value-destructive.

The four modes — January is the optimization frontier

The six events fall into four distinct modes. Understanding them is the difference between "First Saturday is good or bad" and "what makes a particular First Saturday work."

  • Premium Nov 1 — highest AOV ($56.74), premium-priced per-unit basket ($21.25), high acquisition (10.00%). Day-after-Halloween premium-buyer traffic.
  • Holiday traffic Dec 6 — highest gross income of any event ($5,494), tied for most new customers (29), normal basket (2.94). Holiday gift-shopping foot traffic.
  • Basket-driven Feb–Apr — basket inflated 50–66% (4.08–4.42 units), per-unit price collapsed to ~$12 (multi-unit promotion firing), new customers come for the deal.
  • No-event Jan 3 — the outlier. New-customer rate fell below the 7.27% baseline (6.69%). Same discount %, but no basket-building mechanic. The day was a regular Saturday with a discount applied.

January is the only month where the event mechanic didn't fire. It's the highest-leverage optimization target — testing a winter-tailored multi-unit incentive could recover ~10 missing new customers per year for Sacramento.

The owner's concern is empirically correct at Oakland. Five of six Oakland First Saturdays delivered below-baseline new-customer rates (4.55% – 8.60% vs. 9.83% baseline). First Saturday at Oakland is not meaningfully acquiring new customers — it's primarily a discount event for existing customers.

What Oakland First Saturday IS doing

It's not all bad. The event delivers +82% gross income vs. a baseline day ($3,900 vs. $2,133). It's a successful same-store-sales lever for existing customers — they come in, they spend more, they stock up. The discount is steady (20–23% across all six events — the cleanest discipline of the three stores). But it's not earning its keep as an acquisition channel.

Why quarterly, not semi-annual or pause

  1. Oakland's everyday acquisition pipeline is healthy (9.83% baseline = ~16 new customers/day = ~5,800/year). Quarterly First Saturdays still serve as periodic loyalty anchors without straining the calendar.
  2. The Oakland market is hyper-competitive. Local Falcon shows Oakland holding 100% SoLV on three weed keywords — defending visibility matters. Quarterly events keep the marketing rhythm without diluting it.
  3. Frame quarterly events explicitly as loyalty days (e.g., "Bay Area Family Quarter") rather than acquisition events. Setting honest expectations means existing customers don't feel taken for granted — they feel rewarded.

The quarterly trade-off (the key question for ownership)

Moving Oakland to quarterly forfeits ~$31K of event-driven gross income BUT saves ~$16K in event-day discount spend AND opens 8 weekend days to other promotional or operational use. The break-even depends on what those 8 days produce instead. If we redirect them to (a) the GBP photo program, (b) review velocity push, and (c) heritage-led content investment, the everyday acquisition pipeline gains could meaningfully exceed the foregone First Saturday gross income within ~6 months. The hidden Oakland upside: a +0.5pt lift in everyday new-customer rate (9.83% → 10.33%) is worth ~290 additional new customers/year — almost 2× what quarterly First Saturday would still produce.

Why semi-annual, not monthly or pause

  1. WeHo's everyday 19.09% new-customer rate is exceptional — almost 3× Sacramento's. It's driven by Sunset Strip foot traffic + LAPCG legacy authority + 22 years of brand equity. That pipeline is the bigger ROI lever than any monthly promotional event.
  2. WeHo First Saturday's $2,142/event gross income is meaningful but small in chain context. Two events per year is enough to mark them as anchors without diluting the everyday acquisition signal.
  3. Frame the semi-annual events as "WeHo Anniversary" days — leverage the April 20, 2004 opening date and the LAPCG heritage. Two such events per year (April 20 anniversary + a late-fall counterpart) preserves brand storytelling without monthly promo fatigue.

SECTION 09

Decision sequence — what we need from ownership today

  1. Confirm or refine the per-store cadence:
    • Sacramento: keep monthly (with discount-intensity optimization to 22–23% range)
    • Oakland: shift to quarterly (Feb, May, Aug, Nov) starting June 2026
    • WeHo: shift to semi-annual (April 20 + a late-fall date) starting June 2026
  2. Confirm the "Member Appreciation" / "LAPCG Anniversary" framing for the reduced cadence stores. Reframing the cadence as a deliberate brand choice is more important than the cadence itself.
  3. Tie the released cadence to specific Dispenza deliverables. The 18 saved event-days should fund: GBP photo program completion (all 3 stores), heritage-led GBP description rewrites (already drafted), review-velocity push at Oakland, and the Joyleaf-pattern website structural fixes at Sacramento + WeHo. Each maps to specific everyday-acquisition gains.
  4. Lock the cadence test window: June 2026 → November 2026 (6 months). Measure: everyday new-customer rate per store + chain total new customer count. Reassess in December.
If we walk away from this meeting with three "yes" answers, by December the chain delivers more new customers per year at lower discount spend — and reframes its weekend operating model around what each store actually does well.
